Modeling motion

Imagine you are a passenger in a car. You see your friend standing on the sidewalk and wave at her, turning your head to look at her while driving past her. She too turns her head as she waves back. We turn our heads to follow a moving object, so it is not sur­ prising that your friend turns her head—you are in a moving car. But why do you turn your head? After all, your friend is standing still on the sidewalk. However, from your perspective, she is moving away from you. This example helps us understand a very important idea: different observers see the same motion differently. In other words, motion is relative to the observer, the person who is describing the motion. So who is moving in this scenario, you or your friend? The answer is that it depends on who is the observer. The driver of your car, for example, sees you (a passenger in the car) as not moving. This example shows us that when describing motion we need to specify both the object whose motion we are describing (the object of interest) and the person who is doing the describing (the observer).

Motion is a change in an object’s position relative to a given observer during a certain time interval. Without identifying the observer, it is impossible to say whether the object of interest moved. Physicists say motion is relative, meaning that the motion of any object of interest depends on the point of view of the observer.

An observer on Earth sees the Sun move in an arc across the sky. What makes the idea of relative motion confusing at first is that people intuitively use Earth as the object of reference—the object with respect to which they describe motion. If an object does not move with respect to Earth, many people would say that the object is not moving. That is why it took scientists thousands of years to understand why Earth has days and nights. An observer on Earth uses Earth as the object of refer­ ence and sees the Sun moving in an arc across the sky (Figure 2.1a). An observer on a distant spaceship, however, sees Earth rotating on its axis so that different parts of its surface face the Sun at different times (Figure 2.1b).

Specifying the observer before describing the motion of an object of interest is an extremely important part of constructing what physicists call a reference frame. A reference frame includes an object of reference, a coordinate system with a scale for measuring distances, and a clock to measure time. If the object of reference is large and cannot be considered a point-like object, it is important to specify where on the object of reference the origin of the coordinate system is placed. For example, if you want to describe the motion of a bicyclist and choose your object of reference to be Earth, you place the origin of the coordinate system at Earth’s surface, not its center.

When we model objects, we make simplified assumptions in order to analyze com­ plicated situations. Just as we simplified an object to model it as a point-like object in Chapter 1, we can also simplify a process involving motion. What is the simplest way an object can move? Imagine that you haven’t ridden a bike in a while. You probably wouldn’t start by attempting a turn; instead, you would ride in a straight line. This kind of motion is called linear motion or one-dimensional motion.

Linear motion is a model of motion that assumes that an object, considered as a point-like object, moves along a straight line.

For example, suppose we want to model a car’s motion along a straight stretch of highway. We can assume that the car is a point-like object (it is small compared to the length of the highway) and the motion is linear motion (the highway is long and straight).

2.4 Quantities for describing motion

A motion diagram helps represent motion qualitatively. To analyze situations more precisely, for example, to determine how far a car will travel after the brakes are applied, we need to describe motion quantitatively. In this section, we devise some of the quantities we need to describe linear motion.

Time and time interval

People use the word “time” to mean the reading on a clock and how long a process takes. Physicists distinguish between these two meanings with different terms:

Time and time interval Time (clock reading) t is the reading on a clock or some other time-measuring instrument. Time interval (f2 — q) or At is the difference between two times. In the SI system (metric units), the unit of both time and time interval is the second. Other units are minutes, hours, days, and years. Time and time interval are both scalar quantities.

Position, displacement, distance, and path length

We also need to precisely define four quantities that describe the location and motion of an object:

Position, displacement, distance, and path length The position of an object is its location with respect to a particular coordinate system (usually indicated by x or y). The displacement of an object, usually indicated by d, is a vector that starts from an object’s initial position and ends at its final position. The magnitude (length) of the displacement vector is called the distance d. The path length I is how far the object moved as it traveled from its initial position to its final position. Imagine laying a string along the path the object took. The length of the string is the path length.

Figure 2.10a shows a car’s initial position x, at initial time q. The car first backs up (moving in the negative direction) toward the origin of the coordinate system at x = 0. The car stops and then moves in the positive x-direction to its final position Xf. Notice that the initial position and the origin of a coordinate system are not necessarily the same point! The displacement d for the whole trip is a vector that points from the starting position at Xj to the final position at Xf (Figure 2.10b). The distance for the trip is the magnitude of the displacement (always a positive value). The path length I is the distance from x, to 0 plus the distance from 0 to Xf (Figure 2.10c). Note that the path length does not equal the distance.

Free fall

Let’s return to the example that led us to the “discovery” of acceleration: dropping a metal ball onto a foam block (Figure 2.21). Galileo Galilei (1564—1642) did experi­ ments similar to ours in which he dropped objects from different heights and examined the resulting dents in sand. Summary

A reference frame consists of an object of reference, a point of reference on that object, a coordinate system whose origin is at the point of reference, and a clock (Section 2.1)

Time or clock reading t (a scalar quan­ tity) is the reading on a clock or another time measuring instrument. (Section 2.4)

Time interval Ar (a scalar quantity) is the difference of two times. (Section 2.4)

Position x (a scalar quantity) is the location of an object relative to the chosen origin. (Section 2.4)

Displacement d is a vector drawn from the initial position of an object to its final position. The x-component of the displacement dx is the change in position of the object along the x-axis. (Section 2.4)

Distance d (a scalar quantity) is the magnitude of the displacement and is always positive. (Section 2.4)

Path length I is the length of a string laid along the path the object took. (Section 2.4)

Velocity v (a vector quantity) is the ratio of the displacement of an object during a time interval to that time interval. The velocity is instantane­ ous if the time interval is very small and average if the time interval is longer. (Section 2.6)

Speed v (a scalar quantity) is the magnitude of the velocity. (Section 2.6)

Acceleration a (a vector quantity) is the ratio of the change in an object's velocity Av during a time interval Ar to the time interval. The acceleration is instantaneous if the time interval is very small and average if the time interval is longer. (Section 2.7)

Automatic sliding doors The first automatic sliding doors were described by Hero of Alexandria almost 2000 years ago. The doors were moved by hanging containers that were filled with water. Modern sliding doors open or close automatically. They are equipped with sensors that detect the proximity of a person and an electronic circuit that processes the signals from the sensors and drives the electromotor-based system that moves the doors. The sensors typically emit pulses of infrared light or ultrasound and detect the reflected pulses. By measuring the delay between emitted and received pulses, the system can determine the distance to the object from which the pulse was reflected. The whole system must be carefully designed to ensure safe and accurate functioning. Designers of such doors take into account several variables such as typical walking speeds of people and their dimensions.
