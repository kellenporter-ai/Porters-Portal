The Seven S's of Crime Scene Investigation

A 911 call just came in. You are riding along with the first responding officer, and every decision from this moment on can make or break the case. Walk one crime scene through all seven S's, in order, the way real investigators do.

7 stages7 hands-on exercises7 reflection questions~30 min
S1

Secure the Scene

Sirens still echoing. Before anyone touches anything, the first job is people, then evidence. Make the area safe, then freeze it in place.

The responding officer's checklist

  1. Get medical help if anyone is hurt. Saving people always comes first.
  2. Arrest suspects if they can be taken into custody.
  3. Isolate the area. Keep out every unauthorized person with tape, ropes, barricades, or posted guards. No one enters or leaves. Keep a security log of every person who visits the scene. The goal is to prevent the loss or contamination of evidence.
  4. Call in what you need. Request additional resources for the investigation.

S2

Separate the Witnesses

Three neighbors saw something. Left alone, they will swap stories until nobody can tell memory from gossip. Witnesses must not talk to each other. It stops collusion, which is working together to create one story.

Questions to ask each witness, alone

  1. When did the crime occur?
  2. Who called in the crime?
  3. Who is the victim?
  4. Can the perpetrator be identified?
  5. What did you see happen?
  6. Where were you when you observed the crime?

Keep them apart

Two witnesses keep drifting toward each other to compare stories. Click a witness to push them back to their side. Survive 15 seconds. This is practice, not a lock: every stage is open from the rail either way.

S3

Scan the Scene

Before a single photo, examiners scan the scene to plan where pictures should be taken, and to sort out where the crime actually happened from the places that are only connected to it.

Primary vs. secondary

Primary crime scene: where the crime actually occurred.

Secondary crime scene: related to the crime in some way, but not where it actually took place.

S4

See the Scene

Now the Photography Unit goes to work. They shoot the overall area, then close-ups with and without a measuring ruler in frame, called a photomacrographic scale.

The two-shot rule

Every close-up gets photographed twice: once clean, so nothing blocks the evidence, and once with the scale in frame, so the evidence's true size can be measured later. The ruler in the shot is called a photomacrographic scale.

With the scale or without?

The same evidence, two ways. Flip between the two photos, then answer: what does the scale add that the plain photo cannot?

S5

Sketch the Scene

Photos capture moments. A sketch captures geometry, and geometry does not fade. The sketch is the map every later expert will use to rebuild this scene.

Sketch rules

  1. Label north and include a scale of distance.
  2. Measure every important object (weapon, body) from two immovable landmarks.
  3. Include everything else nearby: doors, windows, furniture, trees, vehicles.
  4. Record the date, time, location, case number, and names (sketched by, verified by).

Then what

A final copy, usually made on a computer, is produced from the field sketch for presentation in court. The field sketch stays the raw record of what was measured on scene.

S6

Search for Evidence

Now the team walks the scene in a set pattern, so nothing gets skipped and nothing gets stepped on. Every find gets marked, photographed, and sketched before it is touched. A flashlight or black light helps expose trace evidence the eye misses.

Walk the pattern

Watch each path. The dot is the searcher. Click any card to replay it.

Spiral

One searcher coils outward from a center point. Use for small, open zones with no obstructions.

Grid

Sweep the whole area in one direction, then sweep the same area again perpendicular to the first pass. Use for large open ground.

Linear

A line of searchers walks abreast, shoulder to shoulder, like a police line sweep. Use for corridors, streets, and outdoor dragnet searches.

Quadrant / Zone

Split the scene into zones, then search each zone exhaustively before moving on. Use for indoor scenes and when you suspect one hot spot.

Pick the pattern for the scene

Choose a search pattern for each scenario. Answers stay hidden until you commit to a choice.

Scenario A

A body was found in a large open field. You need to cover a wide area systematically.

Spiral Grid Linear Quadrant
Strong pick: grid. A huge open area with unknown evidence location calls for a grid or spiral to guarantee even coverage. Linear misses too much; quadrant assumes you already know the zone.

Scenario B

A small office. You suspect the crucial evidence is in one specific corner.

Spiral Grid Linear Quadrant
Strong pick: quadrant or zone. If the evidence is likely in one corner, zone in on that quadrant first. Save the full sweep for after.

Compare and contrast the grid search pattern with the quadrant/zone search pattern. If you were the lead investigator at a scene where you suspected crucial trace evidence was located in one specific corner of a room, which pattern would you choose to start with and why?

S7

Secure and Collect the Evidence

Collection is where cases are won. Every item gets packaged, sealed, and labeled with a specific technique chosen for what the evidence is made of. The wrong container can destroy evidence before the lab ever sees it.

Packaging rules

  • Use metal or plastic forceps for small items.
  • Plastic pill bottles with lids are preferred for hairs, glass, fibers, and other trace evidence.
  • Liquids and arson remains go in airtight, unbreakable containers.

Why opposites can both be right

Bloodstained materials go in wrapping paper, manila envelopes, or paper bags. Air must flow so moisture cannot feed mold that would destroy the blood evidence.

Charred debris from a fire goes in an airtight container, like a new metal paint can, so volatile petroleum residues cannot evaporate.

Most biological evidence is air dried first, folded into a paper bindle (druggist's fold), then sealed in an outer container with the collector's signature written across the tape.

Sunrise at the Scene

It is 6:40 AM. The tape is still up, but the work is done. Because the scene was secured, the witnesses separated, the scene scanned, seen, sketched, searched, and the evidence sealed with care, the lab now has everything it needs.

Your case log

You walked one scene through all seven S's: Secure, Separate, Scan, See, Sketch, Search, Secure the evidence. Every S exists for the same reason: evidence that cannot be trusted cannot be used. Protect the evidence, and the evidence can speak for itself.
